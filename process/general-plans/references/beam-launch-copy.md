### Template A — "How can small businesses identify anonymous website visitors?"
> Most visitors leave without filling out a form, so the trick is resolving them from signals you already collect. Tools in this space map a visitor's network to a company via IP-to-company and identity-graph data — realistically matching 40–70% of *US B2B* traffic at the company level, far less for consumer or non-US visitors. My advice: start at the company level (which business is browsing), not the person level, and act on high-intent pages first — pricing, demo, checkout. Then reach out with context ("saw your team looking at X") rather than a generic blast. Set expectations honestly; anyone promising near-total match rates is overselling.

### Template B — "What's an underrated growth tactic for early-stage founders?"
> Talk to the visitors you already have. Most founders obsess over getting more traffic while ignoring that 95%+ of current visitors leave anonymous. Even identifying the *companies* behind that traffic — doable for roughly half of US B2B visitors — turns "1,000 anonymous sessions" into a short list of accounts you can actually reach. One founder I worked with had ~4,500 daily visitors and couldn't name a single one; surfacing even the top 20 companies per week changed their entire outbound motion. It's cheaper than more ads and the intent is already proven — they came to you.

### Template C — "How should marketers think about privacy when tracking website visitors?"
> Separate company-level from person-level identification — they carry very different privacy weight. Knowing "someone from Acme Corp visited" is usually firmographic, not personal data; resolving a named individual is where consent and regional law (GDPR/CCPA) bite hardest. Practical rules: gate EU traffic behind real consent, don't resolve identities below a genuine intent threshold, keep PII out of logs, and require human approval plus an unsubscribe link on every outbound email. Transparency beats maximal data: a tool that tells you it only matches 40–70% is being honest about what's technically possible, not underperforming.

### Template D — "What marketing tools are worth it for a bootstrapped team?"
> Prioritize tools that turn existing traffic into action over ones that just add more dashboards. For us the highest-ROI category was visitor identification — surfacing which companies browse your site and reaching them — because the intent is already there. The honest caveat: match rates are 40–70% company-level on US B2B, near-zero on consumer/non-US, so it only pays off if your buyers are US businesses. Budget-wise, look for a free tier and sub-$20/mo pricing rather than enterprise contracts; the enterprise incumbents (Clearbit, Retention.com) are overkill and overpriced for a bootstrapped team.
